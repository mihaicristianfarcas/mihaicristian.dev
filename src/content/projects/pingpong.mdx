pingpong streams one computer's desktop to another: the picture, the sound, the
keyboard and mouse, controllers, the clipboard. **Ping** is the client you sit
at, **Pong** the host you stream from. It started as a replacement for my own
setup, Apollo on a Windows PC and Moonlight on a MacBook, with one thing they
couldn't give me: every packet travels inside
[pq-boringtun](/projects/pq-boringtun/), the post-quantum WireGuard from my
thesis.

Game streaming leaves nowhere to hide. At 120 frames a second a new frame
leaves the host every 8.3 milliseconds, and a hiccup anywhere along the way
shows up as a stutter on the other screen. Moonlight and Apollo have spent years
getting the details right, so wherever pingpong had to choose how streaming
should behave, it chose what they do, and the code says so. It's still a
separate implementation, written from scratch in Rust, and on the same two
machines it now shows more frames than they do.

## How it works

The host gives each session a display of its own, a virtual one at exactly
the client's resolution and refresh rate, captures it at that rate, and
encodes it in hardware: NVENC on Windows, VideoToolbox on a Mac. Each frame is
cut into packets with Reed-Solomon parity added, so the client can rebuild
what Wi-Fi drops, and the packets leave in small paced groups instead of one
burst that would overflow a wireless queue. When a frame is lost anyway, the
client decodes nothing until a frame that doesn't depend on it arrives, and
asks the host to make the next one a recovery frame. That's Moonlight's rule,
and until pingpong followed it, every lost frame left smeared blocks behind.

Everything rides one encrypted tunnel on one UDP socket per side, inside the
apps, with no network adapter to install. pingpong's own 20-byte header
doubles as the inner IP header WireGuard expects, and encrypting a packet
takes about 0.6 microseconds on an M4 Pro: half a percent of one core at a
full stream. Pairing needs no keys copied by hand. Ping shows a PIN, you type
it into Pong, and the two derive a key from it with SPAKE2 and ML-KEM side by
side, then trade their tunnel keys under it. Away from home, the host
publishes its addresses on the BitTorrent DHT, sealed so only its paired
devices can read them, and both sides punch through their NATs at once.
There's no server in the middle, mine or anyone else's.

Most of a streaming client is the same on every system, so there's one client
and one host, each with a thin native layer: Metal, Direct3D 11, or Vulkan to
put frames on the screen; ScreenCaptureKit, Desktop Duplication, or PipeWire
to capture them.

The newest part lets an AI agent use a host the way you do. It pairs like any
other device, sees the screen, and drives the keyboard and mouse over the same
tunnel, either from Ping's Agents page or from any agent that speaks MCP, like
Claude Code or Codex. You can watch it work, take over, or stop it, and it
asks before anything risky: deleting files, spending money, typing a password.

I built it in four phases between July and September 2026, each with a design
written before the code and measurements taken after it, and all of them are
kept in the repository: what was tried, what it cost, and what changed because
of it. Both apps are now in daily use on my own desk.
