title: 'Starting my master’s'
description: 'On choosing to continue studying when the field keeps changing under everyone’s feet.'
date: 2026-09-21
---

This summer I got admitted to the Cloud, Network Infrastructure and High
Performance Computing master’s programme at Babeș-Bolyai University in
Cluj-Napoca. I applied immediately after finishing my bachelor’s in Computer
Sciences at the same uni. Though it seemed like the obvious next best move in my
relatives’ eyes, I was a tiny bit more skeptical, and had my reasons for it.

For a few years before AI, getting into this faculty basically meant getting a
job and good money assured. Companies came to meet students and hire them
straight from class, almost no-questions-asked. There have also been a lot of
career conversions among middle-aged people at that time. This field was
thriving in Cluj-Napoca (as everywhere else in the world), and people saw
life-changing opportunities in it.

Right now, though, things are different, and most of you reading this in 2026
already struggled with what I’ll now write about.

Major lay-offs happened at big corpos (this can be seen as “coming back to
normality” and a sign that this field was maturing, in a way demanding less
people but more qualified), so the job market ended up saturated with people
with working experience but no job. Then came GenAI, a whole different beast,
which makes work easier and faster, sure, but also renders repetitive, simpler
work obsolete. For an intern or junior dev things were (and still are) looking
rough. Being a student in CS or having a nice little portfolio won’t get you as
far anymore.

These being said, it makes sense to try to pursue higher education, right?
Still, many of my colleagues opted to not go for a master’s degree, and I can
understand why. It’s true that the degree itself pales in comparison to what
skills you can develop on your own to provide great value through your work.
This is still the case, but how easily can you prove it? Before LLMs could
create entire apps by themselves, having a complex portfolio was rare and gave
you insane leverage. Now, getting nice projects on your CV is easier than ever,
and filtering through candidates whose portfolios sound like they have 10+
years of experience in 10 programming languages and architectures, is making
way to a kind of indecision paralysis.

I believe a formal education in the field can, more than ever before, serve as
this key factor in differentiating yourself. It’s something you can rarely fake
all the way. It gives you the means to step out into the wild, to study things
you’d never thought you’d study, to engage in group activities that require
more than technical expertise etc.

This is exactly why I chose to pursue this master’s degree. First, my projects,
bachelor’s thesis and most of all my curiosity drive me in this direction.
Second, the benefit of standing out via formal education in the field. I’m no
prodigy, that’s pretty clear by now, so having the chance to grow professionally
at university, in a direction I’m genuinely passionate about, being a student
for two more years… sounds really good to me.
